WhatsApp Ride-Booking AI Agent

People at a stadium, a concert or a station who do not have the app can still get a car: scan a QR code, chat on WhatsApp, get a price and confirm. In October I rebuilt it from scratch as version 2: a deterministic router in n8n handles every button, list and location, a Claude agent only steps in for free text, and 15 workflows book, track, invoice and escalate. Leadership has approved rolling it out at scale, starting with the airport.

Version 2: rebuilt from scratch

The first version proved the idea. The second is built to be handed over: more stable, faster and clearer for the rider, and maintainable by someone who is not me. The rule behind it is simple: deterministic wherever possible, AI only where it adds something.

For the rider

  • Two languages, chosen by the phone prefix. Spanish-speaking countries start in Spanish and the rest in English, with a button to switch. Free answers follow the language the rider writes in.
  • Origin and destination by text, GPS or a pin on the map, with a visual guide for sending a place other than the current location, and a button to repeat the last destination.
  • 44 Madrid hubs (airport terminals, stations, venues) with their official pickup points. Typing "airport" brings up one button per terminal; stations with several pickup points show a native list.
  • One summary message with a map of the real route and buttons to confirm or change anything; a clear warning, with a link to the app, when a place is outside the service area.
  • Closed choices are always native buttons or lists, so nothing depends on typing. During the ride every message carries View ride, Status, Cancel and Call driver.
  • A payment step. Cash to the driver today; Bizum and bank transfer are already in the flow as "coming soon".

Tracking and rules

  • The rider is never left stranded. If no driver is found, the search goes on for five more minutes at the same price; if there is still nobody, the bot offers the priority product with its real price and arrival time, or the app.
  • Cancelling always asks for confirmation and says the consequence first. Without a driver it offers to move the ride to priority instead.
  • Fair-use rules in code. A late cancellation blocks the number for 48 hours; cancelling with the driver already waiting, or not showing up, for five days. Every block comes with a button to the app.
  • Real-time tracking link, driver assigned, arrival, end of the ride and the invoice, all in the chat.

Inside

  • 15 workflows around one entry router. Address resolution, quoting, creating, cancelling, status and the priority fallback are sub-workflows that the router and the agent share.
  • The model never invents a price or a product. Everything comes from the ride API at that moment. The agent answers in a fixed JSON format, works from a state block that n8n computes on every message, and has at most eight steps per message.
  • Webhook plus reconciliation. Signed ride events arrive by webhook; every minute a reconciliation reads the open rides and creates any event that went missing. Both feed one processor that de-duplicates and decides the message.
  • Guards from day one: de-duplication of incoming messages, an anti-flooding cap, a lock against double clicks when creating a ride, an emergency protocol, and every error posted to Slack with the workflow, the node and a link to the execution.
  • A complete README for the team that runs it, including a summary written to be pasted into an AI assistant as project context.

The agent was never the bottleneck: getting the QR code in front of people was. In October leadership decided to roll it out at scale, and the rollout plan is now owned by the growth and marketing teams.

The rider's side

Scan a QR code, accept the terms, send an origin and a destination by text or live location, walk to a fixed pickup point, see the price and confirm. The agent creates the ride, sends driver updates as the trip progresses, delivers the invoice PDF on WhatsApp and opens a support ticket if something goes wrong.

A real test conversation with the first version, from start to finish: booking a ride to the airport, then cancelling it. Translated from Spanish and redrawn screen by screen; personal data (email, phone, driver, plate and tracking link) is hidden.

The entry point is a QR code. I also designed the launch pack for the channel: 21 print and digital formats in two colourways, from an A4 poster to a counter card and a phone story. Each QR opens the chat with the first message already written, and the number is printed for anyone without a camera at hand.

How the first version was built

  • Deterministic replies. The model does not write the WhatsApp message. It emits a marker, and a switch picks one of seven templates. That removed a whole class of formatting errors, and it means buttons always work.
  • State outside the model. A 15-minute Redis session keeps the last 25 messages, and a data table logs every conversation.
  • Addresses. One tool decides between a places search and reverse geocoding depending on what the rider sent, ranks the candidates and snaps to the nearest allowed pickup hub, then stores it in the session.
  • Rules in code, not in the prompt. The free cancellation window, the trip limit per user, the blacklist and the daily message cap per number are enforced before or after the model, never by asking it nicely.
  • After the booking. A tracker polls the ride every 10 seconds, sends a message at each milestone and asks the rider when something looks wrong. The invoice is read from the mailbox and sent as a PDF.

Results, and what limits them

The system worked end to end from May: on 14 May it took the company's first ride booked, tracked and invoiced with nobody in the loop. A first pilot at a stadium confirmed that it works with real riders. A B2B variant lets corporate accounts ask for immediate rides the same way.

Volume stayed small afterwards because the QR codes were handed out manually. The bottleneck is getting the code in front of people, not the agent, so that is where the next iteration starts. A weekly tracker, rebuilt by a Claude skill from the conversation log and the ride records, follows every conversation from the scan to the drop-off.

Taught at the company academy. This is the first of the two cases in the Level 2 n8n workshop (30 September 2026): three data tables, an AI agent with memory, and every real action, from quoting a price to cancelling a ride, as a sub-workflow the agent calls as a tool.

What I learned

  • Letting the model choose the next step and code choose the message was the single change that made it reliable.
  • Guards are cheap, and they belong in the first release rather than after the first abuse.
