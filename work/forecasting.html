Demand & KPI Forecasting

Before most of the automation work, I spent my first months building models for the Customer Operations team: forecasts of support volume and demand, quarterly KPI targets and a shift planner for a contact centre. The team used them in its weekly reviews.

1. Daily support volume

The team planned staff against a daily forecast of contact volume per audience. I went through many notebook versions in three months. The main steps:

  • NeuralProphet with yearly and weekly seasonality, then with public holidays and a hand-kept events list, then an ensemble of models with different seeds.
  • Back to Prophet, which was easier to explain to the team and much easier to tune.
  • Tuning. First a random search over the prior scales, then a full grid of more than 2,000 configurations, with the winner chosen by its error on the last 28 days, which the model never saw.
  • Bias correction on recent errors, and then the forecast is written to a frozen log before the actuals arrive.

Over a two-month evaluation window, from mid-December to mid-February, it beat the official forecast on daily error.

2. Demand by city

The same approach as a batch, with events per city and a bias correction applied only when the recent gap is moderate (a large gap usually means something changed that a correction factor would hide). Later I rebuilt it as a local pipeline with walk-forward validation: LightGBM with quantile loss on lags, rolling statistics, calendar features and event leads and lags; Prophet with holidays; and SARIMAX. The ensemble weights each model by the inverse of its error and gives a P10 to P90 band, and anomalies are flagged with a robust z-score on the residuals, marked as explained when an event matches.

No single model won everywhere. The ensemble stayed because it was the most stable across cities, not because it was the best in each one.

3. Quarterly KPI targets

Quarterly targets for a whole set of process KPIs used to be set by hand. One notebook now fits a model per KPI, switches to logistic growth with a cap and a floor when the KPI is a bounded percentage, and writes three scenarios (probable, optimistic and pessimistic, from the forecast and its interval) into a formatted sheet per audience.

4. Contact-centre staffing

Erlang C, written with log-gamma terms so it stays numerically stable, gives the minimum agents per interval for the service level. OR-Tools CP-SAT then builds the roster within the working-time rules: shift lengths, one shift per day, weekly hours and rest between shifts. Uncovered demand is a slack variable with a very high cost and overtime a lower one, so the solver prefers any legal roster over an infeasible one.

What I would change now

  • The notebooks were long single cells that only I could run. The later rebuild is a package with a config file, walk-forward folds and a report per series, which is how all of it should have started.
  • Every model should ship with its backtest next to it. The volume forecast had one because of the frozen log; the first vision prototype did not, and it cost me the ability to say how good it was. The rest of that story is in the hybrid vision engine.
