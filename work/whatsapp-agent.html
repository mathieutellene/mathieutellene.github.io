WhatsApp Ride-Booking AI Agent

People at a stadium, a concert or an airport who do not have the app can still get a car: scan a QR code, chat on WhatsApp, get a price and confirm. Behind it is an AI agent for free text and a set of deterministic workflows that book, track and invoice. In October I rebuilt it around a deterministic router, and version 2 is live in a controlled pilot with QR codes at an airport and two venues.

The rider's side

Scan a QR code, accept the terms, send an origin and a destination by text or live location, walk to a nearby pickup point, see the price and confirm. The agent books the ride, sends updates as the trip progresses and delivers the invoice in the same chat.

How it is built

  • The model chooses, code writes. The agent decides the next step, but it does not write the WhatsApp message: it picks one of a small set of templates. That removed a whole class of formatting errors, and buttons always work.
  • State lives outside the model. A short session keeps the recent conversation and every exchange is logged, so the agent never has to remember what it cannot see.
  • Addresses are resolved by a tool, which decides between an address search and the rider's live location and snaps the result to the nearest allowed pickup point.
  • Rules are enforced in code, not in the prompt. Free cancellation windows, limits per user and protection against misuse are checked before or after the model, never by asking it nicely.
  • After the booking, a tracker follows the trip, sends a message at each milestone and asks the rider when something looks wrong.

Version 2: a router first, the model second

The first version gave the model the whole conversation. In October I rebuilt it from scratch: a deterministic router handles every button, list and location, and the model only steps in when someone writes free text. Each capability, such as resolving an address, quoting, booking, cancelling, tracking, invoicing and sending, is its own small workflow that can be tested and changed alone.

  • A test bench plays dozens of scripted conversations against the AI step, without sending anything, before each change.
  • Tracking has a safety net: a reconciliation job compares open trips with the ride platform every minute, in case an event is lost on the way.
  • A way out when things do not go to plan: a priority option when no driver is found, a free cancellation window and the invoice in the same chat.
  • Two languages, and payment by link for riders without a card on file.

Results, and what limits them

It worked end to end from May 2026, when it took the first ride booked, tracked and invoiced with nobody in the loop, and it was shown at the company All Hands in September. The limit on volume turned out not to be the agent but distribution: the QR codes have to reach people at the right place and time. That is what version 2 started with in October, as a controlled pilot at an airport and two venues.

What I learned

  • Letting the model choose the next step and code choose the message was the single change that made it reliable.
  • Guards are cheap, and they belong in the first release rather than after the first misuse.
  • The hardest part of a conversational product is often not the conversation.
  • A router in front of the model makes most of the conversation deterministic, and therefore testable.
