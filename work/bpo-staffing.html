BPO Weekly Staffing Matrix

Every week the outsourced contact centre needs a distribution matrix: how many agents should be working each hour of each day to answer the calls, chats and tickets we expect, and a shift for every person that respects labour rules. That is two problems in one, a queueing problem and a scheduling problem, so I wrote one model for each in Colab, reading the forecast from a Google Sheet and writing the plan back.

From a forecast to a weekly plan

The input is the week's forecast of calls, chats and tickets per hour. The output is what the provider needs to plan its people: agents per hour for every day, a shift for every agent, and a map of where the plan falls short. In between there are two different kinds of maths.

How many agents an hour needs

Contacts do not arrive evenly through the hour, so dividing the workload by the handling time always understaffs. Erlang C, the standard queueing model for contact centres, gives the probability that a contact has to wait for a given number of agents, and from that the share answered within the target time. The notebook adds agents until 90% of contacts are answered within 20 seconds with an average handling time of 195 seconds. It is written with log-gamma terms, so large factorials never overflow.

Then shrinkage: agents are paid for hours they cannot spend on contacts (breaks, training, absence), 15.6% here, so the agents to schedule are the agents needed divided by 1 − 0.156, rounded up. On a quiet night that is the difference between two agents on the phones and three on the roster.

A shift for every person

The roster is a constraint problem, so it runs on Google OR-Tools CP-SAT rather than spreadsheet formulas. Every agent gets one decision per day: a day off or one of 168 shifts (4 to 10 hours, starting at any hour), each a boolean variable. The labour rules are hard constraints:

  • One shift a day of at most 10 hours, and shifts that cross midnight count towards the next day's coverage.
  • At most 44 hours and five working days a week per agent.
  • At least 12 hours between the end of one shift and the start of the next.
  • A day off is a real 24 hours off, also after a night shift that ends in the morning.
  • The week's exceptions, entered before solving: an agent's full day off (holidays, sick leave) or a blocked slot (a medical appointment, a training session).

Uncovered demand is not forbidden. Each missing agent-hour is a variable with a huge cost, so when the team is too small the solver still returns the best legal roster, and says exactly where it falls short, instead of failing. The objective is a priority order written as weights:

# Coverage first, then no overtime, then as few hours as possible
model.Minimize(
    sum(missing_agent_hours) * 1_000_000   # an hour of demand nobody covers
  + sum(overtime_hours)      * 50_000      # every hour of a shift beyond 8
  + sum(normal_hours)        * 1
)
solver.parameters.max_time_in_seconds = 300            # returns the best roster found if time runs out

Everything goes straight back to Google Sheets: the requirements, a global grid with required, rostered and difference per hour plus the estimated service level, one tab per agent with their week, rest periods painted in, and the coverage map.

Scaling to 15-minute slots and 400 agents

The hourly model makes one decision per agent, day and shift option. In 15-minute slots, with starts every 30 minutes and lengths in 30-minute steps, there are 624 shift types a day, and for 400 agents that formulation explodes. So the scaled version asks a smaller question: not who works which shift, but how many people work each shift type each day.

One decision per agent, day and shift

400 agents × 7 days × 625 options (624 shift types or a day off) = 1.75 million booleans, before a single labour rule is written.

How many people per shift type

7 days × 624 shift types = 4,368 integers, with coverage per 15-minute slot and the team's total hours and working days as budgets. Solved to optimal in seconds.

A second, greedy pass turns the counts into names: the longest shifts go first, each to the agent with the fewest hours so far (shuffled, so nobody gets the same pattern every week), checking five days, 44 hours and 12 hours of rest for each person. The export is one row per agent and day, in a filterable sheet, plus a coverage figure for every 15-minute slot. The trade-off is that the counting model cannot see individual rest rules, so in theory the greedy pass can leave a shift without a valid person; with 400 agents that is rare, and the coverage sheet would show it.

What I would change now

  • Weights are policy. A million for a missing agent-hour against fifty thousand for an hour of overtime is a decision about service versus cost, and it belongs to operations, not to whoever writes the code.
  • Erlang C fits calls. Chats, where one agent handles several at once, and tickets, which can wait hours, deserve their own models (concurrency and backlog) rather than being treated as calls.
  • The week's exceptions were typed into a menu in Colab. A tab in the input sheet would have been easier for planners and would leave a record.
  • Size the formulation before the solver. Going from a team to 400 agents was not solved by a faster machine but by asking a smaller question.

The roster and both maps on this page come from a run of the same model on synthetic demand for a synthetic team. The provider, the real team size and real volumes are not shown.
